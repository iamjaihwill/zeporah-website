# Zeporah website: strategy and creative brief

*October 22, 2026. This is the external brand and product website, not the product UI. Its job is to explain what Zeporah is, who it is for, why it matters, and why it can be trusted.*

---

## 1. Brand and positioning summary

### The one sentence

> **Zeporah is the trust-centered coordination platform for serious illness, end of life, and the 13 months after a death — one trusted picture shared by patients, families, and the professionals who care for them.**

### Category

**Trust-centered coordination.** Not a records system, a scribe, a billing tool, or a family chat app. Zeporah sits *between* the family and the organizations serving them, and holds the one thing nobody currently holds: a shared, permissioned account of what is happening, what has been decided, what is waiting, what has been shared, and who owns the next step.

Visitors should place it in seconds by analogy — "the coordination desk for one of life's hardest transitions" — then see it is new because it serves both sides of that desk with two different products.

### The problem we name

Six failures, felt by families *and* care teams:

| Failure | What families feel | What care teams feel |
|---|---|---|
| Repeated storytelling | Reliving it for every new person | Re-taking history already taken |
| Fragmented updates | Hearing different versions from different relatives | Calling five people to say one thing |
| Unclear authority | "Am I allowed to ask for this?" | "Am I allowed to tell them?" |
| Scattered documents | Folders, photos, inboxes, glove boxes | Requests with no context or consent trail |
| Uncertain next steps | Not knowing what matters today | No view of what's stuck |
| Too many calls | Waiting by the phone | Phone tag that crowds out care |

### The promise

**One trusted picture.** Five questions Zeporah always answers, for the right people only:

1. What is happening
2. What has been decided
3. What is waiting
4. What has been shared, with whom, and until when
5. Who owns the next step

### Product truths the site must preserve

- **Two products, one foundation.** A consumer experience (patients, families, proxies, trusted helpers) and an enterprise experience (hospice operations, care guides, clinical leadership; designed to extend to approved hospital and partner organizations). They are intentionally different products — not one interface with a role switcher — connected by one shared trust, coordination, and audit foundation.
- **The span.** Offered at diagnosis, through the patient's death, and to the family for 13 months afterward.
- **The network.** Coordinates across patients, families, hospice teams, hospitals, care professionals, funeral homes, and other *approved* services.
- **Zeporah prepares; people approve.** Zeporah organizes requests, flags what's missing or waiting, and summarizes the record with a source for every line — today by clear, inspectable rules, not an AI model. Every sensitive action — sharing, access, disclosure — is explicit, bounded, and approved by a person, then recorded.
- **What it is not.** Not an EHR, not clinical documentation, not a legal-authority determination, not a funeral or estate service.

### Voice

Precise, humane, protective, quietly strong. Dignity, clarity, steadiness, relief.

| Write | Don't write |
|---|---|
| "Nothing is shared until a person approves it." | "Empower your care journey!" |
| "Two things need you today. Everything else can wait." | "Seamless, AI-powered healthcare." |
| "Held: consent awaiting review. Elena has been told why." | "Error: access denied." |
| "13 months after a death" | "Forever by your side" |

Rules: short declarative sentences; name the specific thing (consent, packet, care guide), never "solutions"; no exclamation marks; no spiritual or sentimental language; no unearned numbers.

### Claims discipline

- **HIPAA:** "Built to meet HIPAA requirements; our formal compliance program — security risk analysis, business associate agreements, independent penetration testing — is underway." Change only when complete.
- **No customer logos, counts, testimonials, or outcome percentages** until they exist. Use "what we measure with you" instead of results.
- **Hospitals and partners:** "designed to extend to," never "used by."
- **AI:** no AI model reads household records today (live AI is a pending founder decision, after security review). Say "Zeporah prepares," never "AI drafts/summarizes." The site may say any AI assistance added later will follow the same prepare-then-approve rule. Don't show "Prepared with Copilot" drafts — in the app they are labelled synthetic examples.

## 3. Homepage narrative and section-by-section copy

The narrative is a single argument: *this transition is chaotic for everyone → Zeporah creates one trusted picture → it does so through two products built for two very different jobs → here is a real workflow moving through both → here is why you can trust it → here is what changes for an organization → talk to us.*

### 3.1 Hero — define the category

- **Eyebrow:** Trust-centered coordination
- **H1:** One trusted picture for life's hardest transition.
- **Subhead:** Zeporah connects patients, families, and care teams from diagnosis, through end of life, and for 13 months after a death — so everyone knows what is happening, what has been decided, and who owns the next step.
- **CTAs:** Request a demo (primary) · See how it works (secondary) · text link "Supporting a loved one? Start here →" to For families.
- **Visual:** "The trusted picture" — a single composed card, not a dashboard. Five labeled rows (Happening / Decided / Waiting / Shared / Next step), each with one line of fictional content, a status chip, and a hairline audit timestamp. Every few seconds one row transitions state (e.g. *Waiting → Approved*), quietly. Labeled "Sample household. Names are fictional."

### 3.2 Problem framing — name the chaos precisely

- **H2:** The hardest transition, coordinated by phone tag.
- **Lede:** Serious illness pulls in a dozen people and a handful of organizations. None of them share the same picture.
- **Body:** the six failures as a 3×2 grid. Each tile: a short title and two one-line halves — *Families:* … / *Care teams:* … This makes the point that both sides suffer the same failure, which is what justifies a shared layer.

### 3.3 How Zeporah works — the foundation

- **H2:** One foundation. Every step accounted for.
- **Two linked devices:**
  1. **The span** — three phases on a horizontal line: *At diagnosis* (plans, preferences, trusted helpers) → *Through end of life* (updates, tasks, care-team coordination) → *13 months after* (practical next steps, documents, bereavement support).
  2. **The trust loop** — four steps: *Prepare* (Zeporah organizes requests, flags what's missing, and summarizes the record with sources) → *Review* (a person checks consent, authority, purpose) → *Approve* (sensitive actions happen only on explicit approval) → *Record* (every decision written to a permanent audit log).
- **Line under the loop:** "Zeporah prepares the work. People make the decisions."

### 3.4 Two experiences, clearly separated

- **H2:** Two products. Built for two very different jobs.
- **Layout:** a deliberate split. Left panel in the consumer visual language, right panel in the enterprise visual language, and a thin shared "foundation" bar beneath both.
  - **For patients and families** — *Calm guidance, one step at a time.* "A private place to see what matters today, share the load with people you trust, and hear from the care team — without managing a system." Mini view: greeting, two priority cards, one message.
  - **For care teams** — *The coordination desk.* "Every household, every open request, and every approval in one accountable view — so your team sees what's stuck and what moves next." Mini view: a dense queue table with status, owner, and age columns.
  - **Foundation bar:** Shared permissions · Explicit approvals · Permanent audit record.
- Each panel links to its page. Neither is "primary"; equal width, equal weight.

### 3.5 Workflow demonstration — one request, two views

- **H2:** One request, from ask to audit.
- **Interactive stepper (5 steps)** showing the same moment in both products side by side:
  1. *Asked* — Elena asks to send her mother's insurance documents. / Request appears on the desk, owner: unassigned.
  2. *Prepared* — "We're getting this ready." / Zeporah drafts a minimum-necessary packet, marked *Draft — needs review*.
  3. *Held* — "This is waiting on a consent review. Here's why." / Policy check: 9 of 10 pass; consent pending → *Hold*, routed to reviewer.
  4. *Approved* — "Approved. Sent to Northside Life, available for 14 days." / Reviewer Dana approves; expiry set.
  5. *Recorded* — "You can see exactly what was shared." / Audit entry with actor, purpose, recipient, expiry.
- This is the main product-demo moment: it proves the two products, the prepare-then-approve rule, and auditability at once.

### 3.6 Trust, privacy, and permissions

- **H2:** Allow, hold, or deny. Never a guess.
- **Body:** default-deny; a family role never unlocks sensitive information on its own; the ten checks every share passes; consent families can withdraw; plain-language reasons when something is held; people decide clinical and legal questions.
- **HIPAA line** (exact wording in §1). Link to Trust.

### 3.7 Enterprise credibility / operational value

- **H2:** What changes for your organization.
- **Four measures, framed as what we track with you** (no numbers): repeated calls · time to acknowledgement · task follow-through · family understanding of next steps.
- **Implementation strip:** Map → Configure → Launch → Review. "Works alongside your EHR. No clinical documentation, no rip-and-replace."

### 3.8 Closing CTA

- **H2:** Bring calm to the families you serve, and order to the work behind it.
- **Primary:** Request a demo. **Secondary for families:** "Ask your care team whether they offer Zeporah."

## 4. Visual and interaction direction

### Overall

Editorial, composed, warm. Think a well-made annual report or a serious magazine, not a SaaS landing page. Generous whitespace, a strong serif voice, restrained color, hairline rules. No purple, no neon gradients, no stock photography of grief or hospital beds, no illustrated mascots.

### Palette (from the brand foundation)

| Role | Color | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Ink | Night Harbor `#17324D` | Headlines, enterprise surfaces, dark sections |
| Secondary text | River Slate `#496A73` | Body copy on light |
| Paper | Parchment `#F5F0E8` / `#FBF9F5` | Consumer surfaces, page background |
| Quiet surface | Fog `#E6ECEA` | Section bands, enterprise panels on light |
| Warm accent | Clay Rose `#B9685B` (text/CTA `#A35648`) | Primary CTA, emphasis, consumer highlights |
| Trust accent | Aged Brass `#B58A45` | Checks, approvals, audit marks only |
| Calm positive | Deep Moss `#5E7463` | Done / approved states |
| Attention | Mulberry `#874B59` | Form errors only — never red alarms |

### Two visual dialects, one system

| | Consumer dialect | Enterprise dialect |
|---|---|---|
| Surface | Parchment, white cards | Night Harbor or Fog, panel grid |
| Type | Fraunces serif headings, generous size | Inter, tight; JetBrains Mono for IDs, timestamps, counts |
| Shape | 18px radius, soft shadow | 6px radius, hairline borders, no shadow |
| Density | One priority per view | Tables, columns, status + owner + age |
| Status | Gentle words ("Today", "Can wait") | Explicit states (HOLD, APPROVED, OWNER) |

Shared across both: Clay Rose for action, Brass for trust, the sparrow mark, the same status vocabulary.

### Photography and product imagery

- **People:** one warm, slightly muted grade on every photo (applied in CSS), and only two shapes — portrait 4:5 and wide 3:2. No clinical procedures, hospital beds, or visible distress. Never place a stock photo where it could be read as the founder or her family.
- **Product:** show focused details (one moment per frame, with a small mono callout label), not whole screens shrunk to thumbnails. Each detail appears on one page only. Captures come from the real app with sample data and are labelled as such.
- **Mark:** a perched sparrow silhouette in Night Harbor; on dark backgrounds, Parchment.

### Typography

Fraunces (300–500) for display; Inter (400–600) for UI and body; JetBrains Mono (400) for enterprise metadata only. Display sizes `clamp(2.6rem, 5.6vw, 4.4rem)`; body 17px/1.6.

### Interactions and demo moments

1. **Hero "trusted picture"** — one row changes state every ~4s with a soft cross-fade. Stops under reduced motion.
2. **Workflow stepper** (home, how it works) — Prev/Next and clickable step labels; family view and desk view update together. Keyboard-operable; current step announced to screen readers.
3. **Two-experience split** — hover lifts each panel slightly; no flipping, no carousels.
4. **Reveal on scroll** — 18px rise and fade, once, disabled for reduced motion.
5. **Nothing autoplaying with sound, no parallax, no scroll-jacking.**

UI in marketing is always *framed*: cropped, labeled as sample, one moment per frame. Never a full-screen screenshot.

### Accessibility

WCAG 2.2 AA: contrast checked for every text/background pair, visible focus rings (Brass), skip link, semantic headings, labeled forms with inline errors, 44px touch targets, works at 320px wide.

## 5. How to present consumer vs enterprise experiences

- **Separate pages, separate dialects, equal standing.** For families is written to a person; For care teams is written to an operator. Neither page borrows the other's visuals except in the shared-foundation strip.
- **For families** speaks in second person and present tense: "You'll see the few things that need you today." It never shows tables, IDs, or queues. It explains: what you'll see, how to bring in helpers, how privacy works for you, what happens after a death (13 months of support), and how to get access (through your care team; always free).
- **For care teams** speaks to outcomes and control: "See every household, what's stuck, and who owns the next step." It shows the desk (queue, approvals, audit), roles (care guides, operations, clinical leadership), what we measure with you, implementation, and EHR boundaries. Hospitals and partners appear as "designed to extend to approved hospital and partner organizations."
- **Where they meet:** the home split, the workflow stepper, How it works, and Trust — always the same request, seen from both sides, held together by the foundation.
- **Never:** a role switcher, a single blended mock, staff language on the family page, or warm softness on the desk.

## 6. CTA and conversion strategy

| Audience | Primary CTA | Where | Secondary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hospice & health-system leaders | **Request a demo** | Nav button, hero, every page end | See how it works |
| Care-team practitioners | Request a demo | Care teams page | Trust page |
| Families | **Ask your care team about Zeporah** | For families, home hero text link, closing CTA | "Suggest Zeporah to your hospice" — the demo form with a "family member or caregiver" role |
| Compliance / IT | Request security documentation | Trust page | Request a demo |

- **One primary action per page.** The nav "Request a demo" is the only persistent button.
- **The demo form** asks only what's needed to schedule: name, work email, organization, role, state, organization size, preferred times, optional focus. It reminds people not to enter patient information and confirms by email. No calendar embed until a scheduling tool is chosen.
- **Families never hit a sales wall.** Their path ends in reassurance and a way to ask their care team, never in a lead form disguised as support.
- **Measure:** demo requests by source page, family-page → "ask your care team" clicks, Trust-page visits before demo requests.
